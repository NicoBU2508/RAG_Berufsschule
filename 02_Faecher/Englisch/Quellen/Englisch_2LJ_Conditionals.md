#### Extrahierter Originaltext für Suche und KI

```text
Language and Grammar
Conditional Sentences
INFO BOX I If-Sentences
There are three types of “if” or conditional sentences:
Type I (likely)
Example: If I find time, I’ll call.
(= At the moment I find no time, but I will probably call later.)
Type II (possible but unlikely)
Example: If I found time, I would call.
(= I don’t expect to find time to call but it is theoretically possible.)
Type III (merely speculative)
Example: If I had found time, I would have called.
(= I didn’t find time and now it’s too late to do more than speculate.)
A mix of type II and III
Example: If they hadn’t married so young, they would be happy now.
INFO BOX II Conditional sentences (provided – when – unless – in case)
Students of English sometimes do not recognize conditional sentences as such because the
condition is not ushered by “if” but by one of the following conjunctions or phrases.
Provided/providing = if and only if
Example: I will only sign the contract, provided you are willing to pay half the costs.
When = at the time at which
Example: he will call ma when the package arrives.
Unless = if … not / except in the case that
Example: Unless we are told otherwise, I think we should just stick to the rules as stated.
In case = for fear that / because … perhaps
Example: Here’s my number in case you need to reach me in a hurry.
```

```text
Informationstechnik – B2 Schuljahr 2022/2023 6
Text zu Aufgabe 2: Leseverstehen (Reception)
Features of Perfect Smart Glasses
After indulging in a veritable charcuterie board of smart glasses released in the last few years,
we can confidently say: We’re not there yet. Current smart glasses aren't capable enough to
replace our smartphones or function as centerpieces for ambient computing experiences such
as our interactive voice assistants. Glimpses of an AR world come in fits and starts, but so far,
that future is still being built at a snail's pace and it's very much a Wild West of
experimentation for both hardware and software.
There are enough good, common ideas shared by all of these devices to craft a sort of recipe
for a better pair of smart glasses. A device that’s a convenient companion, and maybe even a
temporary replacement for your smartphone or laptop, but unlikely to be the sole window
through which you view the world.
The common feature between all the smart glasses is the ability to (for the most part) privately
listen to audio without having wireless earbuds in. Each pair of smart glasses does this slightly
differently, but the basic idea involves speakers on the temples angled toward your ears so
that the audio isn’t audible to the people around you, and your awareness of your
surroundings isn’t interrupted.
All of the smart glasses have access to some kind of smart assistant, whether it is remotely
activating on my phone or directly calling up said assistants. The ideal pair of smart glasses
should be able to offer some hands-free help, whether it’s queueing up your next song, making
a call, or interacting with your other devices nearby. This doesn’t need to go above and beyond
what’s already possible on your phone, and ideally, all of it should happen on device for
obvious privacy reasons.
On-device voice processing seems like a low bar to clear. Consumers are more likely to
welcome smart assistants into their lives if their voice requests aren't stored in the cloud, and
vulnerable to hacking. In that scenario, this assistant may only be used to control the smart
glasses’ functions and send input to your phone.
People use their glasses to express their unique sense of style, and just because smart eyewear
have speakers and a charging port doesn’t mean that’s going to change. The best version of
smart glasses should have as many styles as possible, from material finishes to frame shapes;
the seemingly infinite band and case options for smart watches prove it's doable.
The other path is to embrace pre-existing brands. A logo on the side of a pair of smart glasses
isn’t as embarrassing when it’s from a notable fashion brand. Not everyone’s going to be as
willing to collaborate with certain companies, but if smart glasses can look like something you
already wear? Well, you’ve already won half the battle.
```

```text
English – Malware (Solution to Page 15)

Many PC users consider malware, viruses, spyware, adware, worms, Trojans, etc. as the same thing. While all these infections harm our computers, they are not the same. They are all types of malicious software that each behave differently.
The word malware is a combination of two words “malicious” and “software”. It is a generic term used to describe all of the hostile(1) and intrusive(2) program codes including viruses, spyware, worms, Trojans, or anything that is designed to perform malicious operations on a computer.
```

```text
English – Malware (Solution to Page 16) [Scareware | Spyware | Ransomware | Adware]

1. [Adware] – not truly malware and almost never delivered using one of the methods above. It is software that uses some form of advertising delivery system. Sometimes the way that advertisements are delivered can be deceptive(8) in that they track or reveal(9) more information about you than you would like. Most of the time, you agree to the adware tracking you when you install the software that it comes with. Generally, it can be removed by uninstalling the software it was attached to.

2. [Spyware] – software that monitors(10) your computer and reveals collected information to an interested party. This can be more benign(11) when it tracks what webpages you visit; or it can be incredibly invasive when it monitors everything you do with your mouse and keyboard.

3. [Ransomware] – lately a very popular way for Internet criminals to make money. This malware alters your system in such a way that you’re unable to get into it normally. It will then display some kind of screen that demands some form of payment to have the computer unlocked. Access to your computer has literally to be ransomed(12) from the cyber-criminal.

4. [Scareware] – software that appears to be something legit(13) (usually masquerading(14) as some tool to help fix your computer) but when it runs, it tells you that your system is either infected or broken in some way. This message is generally delivered in a manner that is meant to frighten you into doing something. The software claims to be able to fix your problems if you pay them. Scareware is also referred to as “rogue” software – like rogue antivirus.

Botnet (Blue Box):
Some malware gets onto your computer and appears to do nothing at all. Such malware may have no obvious symptoms, but it has infected your computer along with a group of other computers, forming what is called a “botnet”. This botnet can be directed by an internet criminal to do any number of things including spam delivery and launching DDos-attacks on internet sites. Internet criminals don’t want to do anything direct that may be tracked back to them, so they employ botnets to do their dirty work for them.

Rootkit / Bootkit:
Sometimes you’ll hear the term “rootkit” or “bootkit” used to describe a certain type of malware. Generally, this refers to methods that the malware uses to hide itself deep inside the inner workings of Windows so as to avoid detection.
For maximum protection, make sure your PC is protected with security software that can protect you against malware.

Vocabulary:
8 deceptive = irreführend, trügerisch | 9 to reveal = zeigen, aufdecken | 10 to monitor = überwachen, kontrollieren, beobachten | 11 benign (syn.: harmless) = harmlos, ungefährlich, gutartig | 12 to ransom = freikaufen | 13 legit = echt | 14 to masquerade = sich verkleiden
```
